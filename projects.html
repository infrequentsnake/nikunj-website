Frontier Lab

The Nasdaq didn’t fall 42% in 2008 if you’re Australian. The dollar fell alongside it, so in AUD terms the damage was closer to 26%. That one number bothered me enough that I rebuilt the whole picture in the currency I actually spend.

Frontier Lab optimises across six sleeves: the ASX 200, Nasdaq 100, NIFTY 50, Europe, Japan and gold. Everything is unhedged AUD with dividends included, running from 2000 to 2025 and rebalanced each January.

What I built

  • Four objectives: maximum Sharpe, minimum volatility, the lowest-risk mix that still hits a return you name, and maximum CAGR.
  • Per-asset floors and ceilings, long only, with weights constrained to sum to 100%.
  • An efficient frontier plotted against thousands of random mixes, so you can see how much of the space is genuinely worse than the frontier.
  • Growth of $10,000 on a log scale, plus the return for every individual year.

What it showed

The maximum-Sharpe mix puts 57% into gold and returns 10.9% a year at 8.9% volatility, a Sharpe ratio of 0.81. An equal-weight portfolio of the same six assets manages 8.4% at 13.0% volatility, a Sharpe of 0.40. Gold does that work because it’s negatively correlated with every equity sleeve in the set and still compounded above 10% over the period.

The honest caveat: the weights are fitted in-sample. History doesn’t have to agree with the future, and past covariances aren’t a promise about future ones.

Nasdaq options backtest

I wanted to know whether selling options on the Nasdaq-100 beats simply buying it. So I ran all three approaches from QQQ’s first day of trading in March 1999 through to July 2026. That’s 328 months, with every figure converted to AUD.

What I tested

  • Dollar-cost averaging $1,000 a month, with no timing and no cleverness.
  • Selling a cash-secured at-the-money put every month, taking assignment when it lands, then selling again.
  • Holding 100 shares and writing an at-the-money covered call every month.

What it showed

Dollar-cost averaging put in $328k and finished at $3.57M, or 14.6% a year. The put strategy finished at $13.55M, which looks like a rout until you notice it demanded $2.14M of capital to get there, and asked for it at the ugliest possible moments. There was $316k of forced buying during 2022 alone, while the index was falling. Per dollar actually deployed it returned 14.9%. Thirty basis points over doing nothing, in exchange for 132 assignments and 327 monthly decisions.

The covered call is the one that stings. It collected $204k in premium across the run and still finished behind simply holding those same 100 shares and letting the cash sit idle, 6.3% a year against 6.75%. The shares were called away in 195 months out of 327, and every time I’d have bought them back higher. The Nasdaq’s entire return lives in a handful of violent up-months, and those are exactly the months this strategy sells away for a few hundred dollars.

Takeaway: the boring strategy returned 14.6% a year and never once asked me for money I didn’t have.

CGT calculator

The 2026 to 2027 Federal Budget proposed replacing the 50% capital gains discount with cost-base indexation plus a 30% minimum tax rate, intended to apply to gains accruing from 1 July 2027. Whether that leaves you better or worse off depends on inflation, your marginal rate and how long you hold. It isn’t something you can work out in your head, so I built the thing that works it out.

What I built

  • Contributions that escalate as a percentage of your balance, with a floor and a ceiling, so a savings plan compounds the way a real one does.
  • Every monthly contribution tracked as its own parcel with its own acquisition date, and the 12-month rule applied per parcel.
  • Both proposed transition methods for assets held across the cutover, market value and time apportionment.
  • A side-by-side of what you keep under the old discount against the new indexation rules.

What it showed

In the scenario the calculator opens with, the new rules cost $3,385 more in tax and pull the after-tax return down from 6.42% to 5.69% a year. Indexation only beats the discount when inflation runs high relative to your real return, and at 2.5% CPI it generally doesn’t.

Worth stating plainly: this is analysis tooling, not tax advice. The rules are still proposals and the detail may well change before July 2027.
